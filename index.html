The UK Mosquito Surge, 2026

Why north Cambridgeshire has been overrun for the past month — what the new “London species” story actually is, which mosquitoes are really biting, and what you can do about it.

⚡ The short version

  • You are not imagining it. Summer 2026 was the UK's hottest on record, with six heatwaves. NHS England saw a 35% jump in visits to its bite-and-sting advice pages between May and July, and hospital presentations for insect bites more than doubled in May. This has been a national event, not a local one.
  • The weather sequence is the culprit. A long, dry, 30°C+ spell through July and early August let native mosquito populations build up fast (heat speeds every life-stage), then ~115mm of rain landed in late August and early September — exactly the trigger that floods ditches and launches a mass hatch of floodwater species. Warm + wet + late summer = the worst-case mosquito recipe.
  • The “new species in London” is real but not your problem. On 3 September, UKHSA confirmed Aedes aegypti — the dengue/Zika-carrying yellow fever mosquito — was found breeding in east London homes for the first time in the UK. It is a genuine first and a climate-change milestone. But it was an accidental importation, is 60+ miles away, was confined to a few properties, and its larvae have been destroyed. It is not the mosquito biting you in Ely.
  • Your bites are from native species — chiefly Culex pipiens, Culiseta annulata and, in the Fens, Aedes vexans (the floodwater mosquito). They do not carry serious disease; the risk from UK mosquitoes is very low. The problem is pure nuisance, and it is local: your neighbours are affected too, which means there's a shared breeding source within a few hundred metres — most likely standing water in gardens, water butts, ponds or ditches.
  • You can make a real difference. Mosquitoes breed in small amounts of standing water. Eliminating every water-holding container within ~200m of your houses, twice a week, will genuinely cut the next generation. Full checklist in section 7.

1 A record-breaking summer — the national backdrop

2026 was not just a hot summer — it was the most extreme in the UK's meteorological record. Six separate heatwaves were declared, and records fell all over the map:

38.0°C
Hottest June day on record (Lingwood, Norfolk, 26 June) — 5th hottest day in UK history
35.1°C
Hottest May day on record (Kew Gardens, 26 May), breaking a record from 1922
6
Declared heatwaves (May, June, July, late July, 2 Aug, 9 Aug) — first year 35°C+ in three separate months
3 days
Consecutive red heat warnings (24–26 June) — the first reds ever for Wales, only 2nd time nationally

The dry conditions went with the heat. The Environment Agency declared drought across more than half of England and all of Wales; as of mid-September, three-quarters of England remained in drought and reservoirs were ~20% below normal levels. That dryness is the first half of the mosquito story (section 5) — it let egg and larval populations accumulate without drowning in their own floods, and kept out the cold, wet weather that normally suppresses UK summers.

The bite toll was measurable. NHS England's own website saw 90,206 → 121,976 visits (+35.2%) to its bites-and-stings page between May and July. Hospital visits for insect and spider bites more than doubled in May, the month of the first heatwave, and the National Pharmacy Association reported a clear increase in customers presenting with infected mosquito bites — a reminder that the real medical risk of a UK bite is a scratched-up infection, not a tropical disease.

Expert framing. Dr Lauren Cator (Imperial College, vector ecology) put it plainly for The i: the “home range” — where disease-carrying mosquitoes can breed year-round — has “crept all the way up through the Mediterranean Basin and now up into Europe… it has not as far to go to get into the UK.” Britain is now at the upper end of the thermal limit for species that were tropical a few decades ago.

2 Ely's summer: dry heat, then the deluge

This is actual weather data for Ely (Open-Meteo reanalysis archive), 1 July – 16 September 2026. Look at the shape of it: seven near-dry weeks of 30°C+ heat, then four wet weeks in a row. That is not normal — it's the exact sequence that produces a late-summer mosquito explosion.

Weekly totals for Ely, ISO weeks 27–38 (late June to mid-September 2026). Bars: rainfall (mm); line: warmest day of the week (°C).
  • Weeks 27–33 (late June – mid-August): almost zero rain. Less than 13mm total over seven weeks. The warmest days hit 31.3°C (9 July), 32.1°C (29 July) and 32.9°C (13 August). Hot and bone dry — eggs laid in early summer matured rapidly, and the absence of flooding meant no mass drowning of larvae.
  • Week 34 (17–23 Aug): 34.5mm — the first big soak.
  • Week 35 (24–30 Aug): 38.9mm, the wettest week, including 16.9mm on 27 August in a single day, with temperatures still 19–25°C.
  • Weeks 36–37 (31 Aug – 13 Sept): another 40.7mm spread over two mild weeks.

In plain terms: ~115mm of rain in the five weeks from 17 August to 16 September, arriving while the weather was still warm enough for mosquitoes to stay active. Female mosquitoes had been building up all summer; that rain then flooded every ditch, drain, water butt and container at once — and a floodwater species like Aedes vexans is essentially waiting for this trigger. What you're experiencing right now is the hatched generation coming out of that water.

3 The “new species in London”: Aedes aegypti

You found this yourself, so let's do it properly. On 3 September 2026, the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) confirmed that Aedes aegypti — the yellow fever mosquito — had been found breeding at residential properties in east London. The key facts:

  • First breeding in the UK. This is the fourth modern detection of the species in Britain, but the first time eggs/larvae have been found. Earlier sightings (2023–2025) were single adults that died without breeding.
  • Found by citizen science. The initial report came through UKHSA's Mosquito Watch scheme — people sending in photos of suspicious biting mosquitoes.
  • What it does. Ae. aegypti is native to North Africa and lives across the tropics. It is the main vector for dengue, chikungunya, Zika and yellow fever. (Notably it does not carry malaria.) It bites at dusk and dawn and indoors — a very different pattern from most native UK species.
  • How it got here. Almost certainly accidental importation — hitchhiking in vehicles, baggage or potted plants. UKHSA's head of medical entomology, Dr Jolyon Medlock, was explicit that they are believed to be “accidental importations”.
  • Response. Larvae destroyed; a 300-metre radius inspected for standing water and treated; adult traps deployed; monitoring ongoing for several weeks under the National Contingency Plan for Invasive Mosquitoes.
  • Risk assessment: very low. No human infections; no mosquitoes or larvae found in nearby public areas; and the UK climate is still too cold for the species to survive winter. It has never overwintered anywhere in northern Europe — its only European strongholds are the Mediterranean. Oxford's Dr César López-Camacho called it an “early warning rather than an immediate public-health threat”; LSHTM called it a “milestone event”.
What it means — and doesn't mean. It does not mean London (let alone Ely) now has dengue-transmitting mosquitoes. For local transmission you'd need: a person infected with dengue/Chik/Zika (usually returning from abroad) → bitten by Ae. aegypti → the mosquito surviving 10–14 days → biting someone else. With the London population eradicated, that chain is closed. What it does mean is that incursions are becoming more common and that a species that historically couldn't even survive a UK summer is now completing a full breeding cycle here. UKHSA expects this to happen again — and to get more survivable — as the climate warms. For context: in late August 2026, France reported locally transmitted dengue, West Nile and chikungunya cases across the country including Paris — the scenario the London finding is a warning about, one step further along.

And the Asian tiger mosquito?

Aedes albopictus (the “tiger mosquito”, another dengue-capable daytime biter) has had its own run of near-misses: eggs found in Kent in 2016, again 2017–2019, and in August 2024 at an M20 service area — every time followed by surveillance that found no established population. It is the next most likely candidate for a UK foothold, and UKHSA's ovitrap surveillance is concentrated on exactly the kind of sites (ports, service stations, distribution hubs) where it would arrive.

TL;DR for the neighbour conversation: the London story is a genuine climate milestone, but it's geographically isolated, already controlled, and biologically unrelated to what's flying in your garden this September. Don't let it scare you into thinking you're at risk of dengue. Do let it frame the bigger picture in section 8.

4 Who is actually biting you

Over 30 mosquito species are native to Britain. The ones that matter for a house in the Fens, summarised from LSHTM's June 2026 briefing (Dr Mojca Kristan, medical entomology):

Floodwater mosquito Aedes vexans Prime suspect

Breeds on floodplains, river margins and low-lying land where water levels fluctuate. Its eggs survive desiccation for months and hatch en masse when the water returns — precisely what happened in the Fens when the August/September rain came after the dry spell. Active at dusk and dawn; aggressive and persistent. In a landscape like ours, this is the species that turns a wet month into a swarm.

Common house mosquito Culex pipiens Garden biter

Breeds in ponds, ditches, water butts, buckets, bird baths — almost anything holding water. Overwinters as an adult, so the first warm days of spring produce early-season bites (2026 saw an unusually early spring spike, partly because the start of the year was one of the wettest on record). Mostly a night-time biter; the classic “mosquito in the bedroom”.

“London Underground” mosquito Culex pipiens f. molestus Urban

A subspecies that has moved into warm urban infrastructure — sewers, pipes, drains, cellars. Active even in winter. Less likely to be your main problem in the Fens, but worth knowing if you get bites indoors on a cold day in January.

“House” mosquito Culiseta annulata Persistent biter

One of the most common UK species and a genuinely persistent biter: adults don't true-hibernate and start seeking blood when temperatures rise above ~7°C. Likes ponds, ditches, water butts and cisterns. A named driver of the late-season activity UK experts flagged this week.

Marsh mosquito Anopheles plumbeus Moving into gardens

Historically a tree-hole and woodland breeder, but recently spreading into urban and garden environments (bird baths, containers). A persistent nuisance biter, and the UK species most studied as a potential West Nile vector — no UK human cases, though West Nile was first detected in UK mosquitoes (Nottinghamshire) in 2025.

River marsh mosquito Anopheles claviger Ditches & drains

Overwinters as a larva in ditches, streams, temporary pools and even garden tanks and rain barrels; adults can appear from late winter. A contributor to early-season bites in fen country.

Which are “invasive”? None of the above — they're all British. The invasive species to watch (Ae. aegypti Ae. albopictus) are small, black, and marked with white — if you actually see one, that's a report-to-UKHSA event, not a garden problem. Native UK mosquitoes do not transmit dengue, Zika, chikungunya or yellow fever.

5 Why the surge is happening now — the mechanism

Mosquito population size is the product of three things: development speed, survival, and breeding water. 2026 delivered a perfect storm of all three, in sequence:

  1. Heat accelerates every stage. Warmer temperatures speed egg, larval and pupal development — in the mid-20s, Culex and Anopheles larvae hatch to adult in days rather than weeks. More generations fit into one season, and each generation is bigger. Dr Yasser Qureshi (mosquito-behaviour researcher) to The Independent: “Higher temperatures speed up egg, larval and pupal development… humidity helps them survive longer.”
  2. The dry heat phase let populations accumulate. July–early August was hot and nearly rain-free (section 2). No cold snaps, no mass flooding to drown larvae — just compounding generations in whatever water was available (garden ponds, ditches, water butts).
  3. The rain then triggered a floodwater hatch. The late-August/early-September downpours (~115mm in five weeks) flooded fenland ground and ditches at once. For Aedes vexans, that water returning to dry floodplain eggs is a mass-hatch trigger — the eggs are adapted to wait out desiccation and emerge all together when it floods. Multiply that by a summer's worth of accumulated Culex/Culiseta adults still active in mild temperatures, and you get the current situation: huge numbers of adults emerging simultaneously in the last warm weeks of the year.
  4. Timing amplifies it. This generation is biting in September, when people are spending long evenings outdoors, when houses are less ventilated, and when a “normal” September bite count is near zero — so the contrast feels dramatic. It is: experts at LSHTM have explicitly warned that mild, humid September conditions keep native populations elevated well into autumn, and the Met Office is forecasting a wetter, stormier autumn (El Niño influence), which could support another partial generation.
The one-line explanation for the neighbour: “The heatwave let them build up all summer, and the rain in late August flushed the ditches full of newly hatched mosquitoes all at once. It's the weather, and it's all over the country — the NHS bite-page visits went up a third this summer.”

6 The Fenland factor — why here, why this hard

The Fens are a mosquito factory by construction. The landscape we live on is below or level with sea, held in check by one of the most complex drainage systems in Europe — rivers, internal drainage boards, main drains and ditches that have been managed (and re-managed) continuously since the great 17th-century drainages. For centuries, that same standing water was the source of “ague” (malaria) in Fenland — it was drained out of the county, not because mosquitoes left, but because the breeding water was removed from the ground people lived on.

What that means in 2026:

  • Maximum breeding surface. Every ditch, main drain, silted channel, pond and flooded field is a nursery. A wet August in the Fens is not a weather event — it's a mosquito hatchery filling to the brim.
  • Floodwater species dominance. Aedes vexans is essentially a fenland specialist — it breeds on the margins of fluctuating water exactly where our drainage meets the fens. In drier counties with rockier ground, a wet month is a nuisance; here it's a population reset button.
  • Local, not exotic. The fact that your house and the one next door are both affected strongly points to a shared local source — a ditch, drain, pond or cluster of gardens within a couple of hundred metres. Adult Culex and A. vexans don't fly far; this is a neighbourhood-scale problem, which means it's also a neighbourhood-scale fix (section 7).
  • The drainage paradox. When the internal drainage boards run channels high (or a silted channel holds water), that's mosquito habitat. Low water levels and fast flow are not. If a particular drain or ditch is the obvious local hotspot, it's worth a call to the Fenland Internal Drainage Board / Environment Agency — but the bigger lever is the containers and ponds around the houses themselves.

7 What actually works

A. Kill the breeding (the real solution)

Mosquitoes only need one capful of still water for 4–7 days. Walk the perimeter of both houses and clear everything:

  • Water butts: fine mesh lids (or a bit of Bti — see below). A lid with a gap smaller than a coin stops most.
  • Plant saucers and vases — empty and wipe weekly.
  • Buckets, tubs, bins, broken pots, wheelie bins — tip and turn upside down.
  • Children's paddling pools, ball pools — empty after use, or treat (below).
  • Bird baths: change water every 2–3 days, or scrub (eggs stuck to the side otherwise survive a change of water).
  • Blocked drains, downpipes, old tyres, leaf litter holding water — unblock and clear.
  • Ponds: the hard one. Options: add a few fish (even small goldfish eat larvae), steepen the banks, or treat with Bti (see below). A pond is a season-long nursery — if both houses share the view of one, that's likely your main source.
Bti — the clean weapon. Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis is a bacterium sold as granules, wafers or liquid (e.g. “Mosquito Dunks”) that kills mosquito larvae and nothing else of consequence (safe for fish, mammals, pets; it's been used in garden ponds for decades). Drop a wafer in water butts, ponds and bird baths before a wet spell and you remove the nursery without chemistry. UKHSA and public-health guidance specifically endorse standing-water removal; Bti is the standard adjunct for water you can't remove.

Cadence matters: do this twice a week while it's warm. Eggs can sit in dried containers and hatch when the next rain fills them — the autumn forecast suggests there will be next rains.

B. Stop the bites (personal)

  • Repellent that works: DEET (20–50%), picaridin (10–20%) or IR3535 on exposed skin. These are the ones with real efficacy data; citronella “mosquito bits” and candles are essentially placebo for anything beyond a two-metre radius.
  • Permethrin on clothes (treat shirts/trousers before a long evening out; not for skin; wash out before re-wearing).
  • Timing: Aedes vexans is a dusk/dawn biter; Culex peaks night-time. If you can move big outdoor meals earlier or later, you dodge the peak.
  • Housing: fine mesh on any open window/vent that's getting incursions; a bedroom fan at medium genuinely interferes with their flight; screens on the bedroom window are the highest value-per-pound fix if bites are keeping you up at night.
  • Bite care: antihistamine cream or a cold compress for itch. If a bite gets hot, red, spreading or oozing — that's a secondary infection, see a GP. If you've recently travelled to a dengue/Zika/chikungunya area and develop fever within ~2 weeks of returning, tell your GP (not a concern for a home-grown bite, but the standard precaution).
Skip the noise. Ultrasonic repellers, “mosquito magnets”, bug zappers and citronella candles do not meaningfully reduce bites or populations. The zapper kills the odd straggler (and mostly harmless moths and midges); it does nothing at the source. Budget for mesh, Bti and DEET.

C. Help with surveillance (free, 2 minutes)

If you see something that looks like a small black-and-white Aedes (a single straight white stripe = tiger mosquito; a curved lyre shape = Ae. aegypti), take a clear photo and report it via UKHSA's Mosquito Watch scheme (gov.uk/guidance/mosquitoes-how-to-report) — photo, or a specimen frozen for 10 minutes in a crush-proof container. That's how the London finding was made. Even native-species photos help UKHSA map distributions.

8 The bigger picture

Strip away the 2026 specifics and the direction of travel is clear, and it's well-evidenced rather than speculative:

  • UK mosquitoes are becoming more active for longer. The early-2026 bite spike, the extended summer season, the September warning from LSHTM — all consistent. Warmer springs mean earlier first bites; warm autumns mean a final generation that would have been impossible in a 2015 September.
  • Invasive incursions will keep coming. UKHSA says it explicitly: expect continued incursions of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus, and increased reports of them surviving. The London breeding event is the first proof that a full life cycle is now possible in southern England in a hot summer. The Mediterranean is the current southern limit of their comfort; that line is moving north — the UK is now within a few hundred km of it, and importation pressure (trade, travel, potted plants) is at all-time highs.
  • Europe is already there. France's late-August 2026 locally-transmitted dengue, West Nile and chikungunya cases (including Paris) show the end state: local transmission in a temperate country. The UK is behind on the same curve, and West Nile detection in UK mosquitoes (Nottinghamshire, 2025) was the first domestic warning shot.
  • What it would take for real risk. An invasive Aedes surviving UK winters (establishment) + a sufficient human reservoir of the relevant virus + a long warm season. None of the first two are true yet. UKHSA's assessment — “very low” risk today, rising over decades, not years — is the honest one. The practical consequence isn't panic, it's the same standing-water hygiene that solves your September problem, done consistently.
Bottom line for Whichford: 2026 was a freak-perfect year for mosquitoes — record heat + drought + a late deluge + a Fenland landscape full of floodwater eggs. It's a nuisance event, not a disease event. Clear the water, treat the ponds with Bti, mesh the bedroom, and expect the pressure to ease as the nights cool below ~15°C. And keep an eye on UKHSA if the London situation escalates — though the odds of it reaching the Fens this year are essentially zero.
